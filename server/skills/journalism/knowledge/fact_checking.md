# Fact-Checking Guide

Methods and resources for verifying information in journalism.

---

## Why Fact-Checking Matters

- **Credibility**: One error undermines trust in all your work
- **Accountability**: Journalists are gatekeepers of public information
- **Legal Protection**: Errors can lead to defamation claims
- **Ethical Obligation**: Truth is journalism's core value

---

## The Verification Process

### Before Publishing

1. **Verify the source**: Who is providing this information?
2. **Verify the claim**: Is the statement accurate?
3. **Verify the context**: Is it presented fairly?
4. **Verify supporting evidence**: Do the facts hold up?
5. **Verify your own work**: Have you made any errors?

---

## Verifying Sources

### Assess Source Credibility

**Questions to Ask**:
- What is the source's expertise?
- What is their track record?
- Do they have an agenda?
- How did they obtain this information?
- Can they be identified?

### Primary vs. Secondary Sources

**Primary Sources** (Prefer these):
- Original documents
- Direct witnesses
- Official records
- First-hand accounts
- Raw data

**Secondary Sources** (Use cautiously):
- News reports
- Wikipedia
- Social media posts
- Hearsay
- Press releases

### Human Sources

**Verify Identity**
- Confirm they are who they claim to be
- Check credentials
- Verify employment/position
- Cross-reference with public records

**Assess Motivation**
- Why are they talking?
- What do they gain?
- Are they reliable?
- Any history of false claims?

### Institutional Sources

**Evaluate Credibility**:
| High Credibility | Medium Credibility | Lower Credibility |
|------------------|--------------------| ------------------|
| Government statistics agencies | Think tanks | Advocacy groups |
| Peer-reviewed journals | Trade associations | Partisan organizations |
| Major universities | Corporate reports | Anonymous sources |
| Courts and official records | Professional polls | Social media claims |

## Verifying Claims

### The Two-Source Rule

- Major claims should be verified by at least two independent sources
- Independent means they didn't get the information from each other
- More sensitive claims may require more sources

### Verification Techniques

**Cross-Reference**
- Check claim against multiple sources
- Look for original source of widely repeated claims
- Be suspicious if everyone cites the same source

**Seek Primary Documents**
- Don't rely on others' summaries
- Read the actual report, study, or document
- Check if quotes are accurate and in context

**Check the Math**
- Verify statistics yourself
- Calculate percentages independently
- Check that numbers add up

**Call Experts**
- Ask subject-matter experts to evaluate claims
- Get independent analysis of technical material
- Be aware of expert biases

## Verifying Specific Types of Information

### Statistics and Data

**Questions to Ask**:
- What is the source of the data?
- How was it collected?
- What is the sample size?
- Is it representative?
- What are the margins of error?
- Is it current?
- Are comparisons valid?

**Red Flags**:
- No methodology provided
- Unclear source
- Cherry-picked data
- Misleading comparisons
- Outdated information

### Quotes

**Verification Steps**:
1. Check recording/notes against transcription
2. Ensure quotes are not taken out of context
3. Verify the speaker actually said it
4. Confirm the date and setting

**When Using Old Quotes**:
- Verify the original source
- Check if the person's views have changed
- Provide context on when it was said

### Photos and Videos

**Basic Checks**:
- Reverse image search (Google Images, TinEye)
- Check metadata (date, location, device)
- Look for signs of manipulation
- Verify location matches claim

**Advanced Verification**:
- Geolocate using landmarks
- Check weather/lighting against claimed date
- Analyze shadows and reflections
- Use forensic tools for manipulation

**Tools**:
- Google Reverse Image Search
- TinEye
- InVID (video verification)
- FotoForensics
- Jeffrey's Exif Viewer

### Social Media Content

**Verification Steps**:
1. Verify the account is real (not impersonation)
2. Check account history and credibility
3. Look for original post (not screenshots)
4. Verify content hasn't been manipulated
5. Contact the poster if possible

**Red Flags**:
- New account with few followers
- Account created around news event
- Only shares viral/inflammatory content
- Won't verify identity
- Content only exists as screenshot

### Viral Claims

**Approach with Skepticism**:
- The more dramatic, the more verification needed
- Check if professional journalists have verified
- Look for original source
- Check fact-checking sites

## Common Errors to Avoid

### Confirmation Bias

- Don't seek only evidence that supports your hypothesis
- Actively look for contradicting evidence
- Be willing to change conclusions based on evidence

### False Balance

- Not every issue has "two equal sides"
- Scientific consensus shouldn't be balanced with fringe views
- Verify the credibility of all perspectives

### Over-Reliance on Authority

- Even experts can be wrong
- Check their sources too
- Look for consensus, not single voices

### Lazy Attribution

- "Studies show" — which studies?
- "Experts say" — which experts?
- "Sources report" — be more specific

### Outdated Information

- Check when statistics were collected
- Verify current status of ongoing situations
- Look for more recent data

## Fact-Checking Workflow

### Pre-Publication Checklist

**Names and Titles**
- [ ] Spelling verified against official sources
- [ ] Titles current and accurate
- [ ] Age verified (if included)
- [ ] Affiliations confirmed

**Dates and Times**
- [ ] All dates verified
- [ ] Day of week matches date
- [ ] Timelines are consistent
- [ ] Historical dates checked

**Numbers**
- [ ] Statistics traced to original source
- [ ] Math verified
- [ ] Numbers consistent throughout
- [ ] Context provided for statistics

**Quotes**
- [ ] Verified against recording/notes
- [ ] Context preserved
- [ ] Attribution accurate
- [ ] Speaker confirmed quote

**Claims**
- [ ] Major claims multi-sourced
- [ ] Sourcing is appropriate
- [ ] Counterclaims considered
- [ ] Qualifications included where needed

**Media**
- [ ] Photos/videos verified
- [ ] Captions accurate
- [ ] Permissions/credits secured
- [ ] No misleading cropping/editing

## When You Make Mistakes

### Immediate Steps

1. Acknowledge the error
2. Correct it promptly
3. Note the correction clearly
4. Preserve record of change

### Correction Format

"**Correction**: An earlier version of this article stated [error]. In fact, [correct information]. This article has been updated."

### For Online Articles

- Add correction at top or bottom
- Note what was changed
- Keep correction visible
- Don't delete the error silently

## Key Principles

> "If your mother says she loves you, check it out."
> — City News Bureau of Chicago (traditional journalism saying)

> "The absence of evidence is not evidence of absence."
> — Verify before concluding something isn't true

> "When in doubt, leave it out."
> — Don't publish what you can't verify

The best fact-checking happens before publication, not after. Build verification into your reporting process from the start.
