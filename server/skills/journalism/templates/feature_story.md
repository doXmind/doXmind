## The Opening Scene (The "Lede")

[Set the scene with vivid, sensory detail. Place the reader in a specific moment. Use narrative techniques: scene-setting, character introduction, action, dialogue.]

**Techniques**:
- **Anecdotal**: Start with a specific person's story
- **Scenic**: Describe a place or moment in detail
- **Narrative**: Begin in the middle of action
- **Descriptive**: Paint a picture with sensory details

**Length**: 2-5 paragraphs

**Example Opening**:
"Maria Santos arrives at the community garden at 5:30 every morning, before the Texas sun turns the air to soup. She walks the rows slowly, her weathered hands brushing the tomato leaves, checking for the tiny green caterpillars that can destroy a month's work in a single night.

At 73, she has been tending this quarter-acre plot for 15 years—longer than anyone else in the neighborhood. She was here before the new apartments went up, before the coffee shops arrived, before the word 'gentrification' became part of the local vocabulary."

## The Nut Graf

[After the opening scene, pull back to explain what this story is about and why it matters. This is your thesis—the "so what" of the piece.]

**Elements**:
- What is this story really about?
- Why should readers care?
- What larger themes does it explore?
- What will readers learn?

**Example**:
"Santos is one of a dwindling number of urban gardeners fighting to preserve green spaces in neighborhoods being transformed by development. Across the country, community gardens that once thrived in vacant lots are disappearing as land values soar—and with them, a vision of urban life that values community over commerce."

## The Ending (The "Kicker")

[Return to your main character or opening scene. Provide closure while leaving readers with something to think about.]

**Ending Techniques**:
- **Circle back**: Return to opening scene with new meaning
- **Looking forward**: What comes next
- **Poignant moment**: A small detail that captures the whole
- **Quote**: A powerful quote that encapsulates the theme
- **Reflection**: The writer's observation (in appropriate contexts)

**Example Ending**:
"As the sun sets over the garden, Santos packs up her tools. Tomorrow, she'll be back at 5:30, checking her tomatoes, pulling weeds, doing what she's done for 15 years. She doesn't know how many more seasons she has—in this garden, or anywhere.

'But that's true for all of us,' she says, looking over the rows of vegetables, the sunflowers standing tall against the fence. 'You plant seeds. You do your best. And you hope.'"

## Structural Options

### Narrative Arc
```
Opening Scene
     ↓
Nut Graf (What this is about)
     ↓
Background/Context
     ↓
Complication/Conflict
     ↓
Rising Action
     ↓
Climax/Turning Point
     ↓
Resolution
     ↓
Kicker
```

### Braided Narrative

Alternate between:
- Story A (main narrative)
- Story B (parallel story or historical context)
- Analysis/context

### Thematic Sections

Organize around themes rather than chronology:
1. Theme 1: [Topic]
2. Theme 2: [Topic]
3. Theme 3: [Topic]
4. Synthesis and conclusion

## Feature Writing Techniques

### Show, Don't Tell

**Telling**: "Maria was dedicated to the garden."

**Showing**: "Maria arrives at 5:30 every morning, before the Texas sun turns the air to soup. She walks the rows slowly, her weathered hands brushing the tomato leaves."

### Sensory Detail

Engage all five senses:
- Sight: Colors, shapes, movement
- Sound: Dialogue, ambient noise
- Smell: Distinctive scents
- Touch: Textures, temperatures
- Taste: When relevant

### Scene Construction

Every scene needs:
- Setting (where and when)
- Characters (who)
- Action (what's happening)
- Purpose (why this scene matters)

### Pacing

- Short paragraphs and sentences = faster pace, tension
- Longer paragraphs = slower pace, reflection
- Vary rhythm throughout the piece
- Use white space strategically
