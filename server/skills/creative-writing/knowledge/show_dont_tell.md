# Show, Don't Tell

The fundamental principle of immersive fiction writing.

---

## What It Means

**Telling**: Informing the reader directly about a character, emotion, or situation.

**Showing**: Creating experiences that allow the reader to deduce information.

### Example

**Telling**: "Sarah was angry."

**Showing**: "Sarah's jaw tightened. She slammed the folder onto the desk, scattering papers across the floor."

The reader *feels* Sarah's anger rather than being *told* about it.

---

## Why It Matters

### Reader Engagement
- Showing invites readers to participate
- Readers draw conclusions = deeper engagement
- Creates "aha" moments of understanding

### Emotional Impact
- Shown emotions feel authentic
- Readers empathize through experience
- Avoids the "so what?" reaction to told emotions

### Trust
- Shows respect for reader intelligence
- Avoids condescension
- Builds confidence in the narrative

---

## When to Show

### Emotions and Internal States

**Tell**: "He was nervous."

**Show options**:
- **Physical sensation**: "His stomach churned."
- **Action**: "He checked his watch for the fifth time in two minutes."
- **Body language**: "His foot tapped an erratic rhythm against the floor."
- **Thought pattern**: "Had he said the wrong thing? He replayed the conversation, searching for the misstep."

### Character Traits

**Tell**: "Maria was generous."

**Show options**:
- **Action**: "Maria pressed a twenty into the barista's tip jar, then pretended to study the menu board."
- **Dialogue**: "'Keep the change,' Maria said. 'Buy yourself lunch.'"
- **Pattern**: Show her giving repeatedly throughout the story.

### Relationships

**Tell**: "They had a tense relationship."

**Show options**:
- **Body language**: "Tom edged toward the door. His father's eyes tracked him like a predator."
- **Dialogue**: "'I see you're wearing that shirt.' His father's gaze swept over him. 'Again.'"
- **Reaction**: "Sarah flinched at her mother's voice in the hallway."

### Setting/Atmosphere

**Tell**: "It was a depressing room."

**Show options**:
- **Sensory details**: "Fluorescent lights buzzed overhead, casting everything in a sickly yellow. The carpet, once beige, had grayed to the color of old dishwater."
- **Significant details**: "A single motivational poster clung to the wall, its corner peeling down like a defeated flag."

## How to Show

### Use the Five Senses

| Sense | Example |
|-------|---------|
| **Sight** | "The walls were the green of hospital scrubs." |
| **Sound** | "The silence had a weight to it, pressing against her ears." |
| **Smell** | "The room smelled of old coffee and broken promises." |
| **Touch** | "The paper was damp, soft at the edges." |
| **Taste** | "Fear tasted like pennies in his mouth." |

### Physical Sensations for Emotions

| Emotion | Physical Sensations |
|---------|---------------------|
| **Fear** | Cold sweat, racing heart, dry mouth, tight chest, trembling |
| **Anger** | Heat rising, clenched jaw, tight fists, pounding heart |
| **Joy** | Lightness, warmth spreading, inability to stop smiling |
| **Sadness** | Heavy chest, throat tightening, stinging eyes |
| **Anxiety** | Churning stomach, restlessness, shallow breathing |
| **Love** | Warmth, ache in chest, heightened awareness of the other |
| **Shame** | Heat in face, avoiding eye contact, wanting to shrink |
| **Surprise** | Sharp intake of breath, frozen moment, widened eyes |

### Action and Behavior

Characters reveal themselves through:
- What they do
- What they choose not to do
- How they react to situations
- Their habits and routines
- What they notice

**Example**:
Instead of "John was meticulous," show John:
- Aligning pens on his desk
- Refolding a napkin
- Noticing a crooked picture frame

### Dialogue and Subtext

What characters say—and don't say—reveals them.

**Tell**: "She didn't want to talk about it."

**Show**:
"'How was the meeting with your father?' Tom asked.

Sarah turned toward the window. 'Did you move the car this morning?'"

### Specific Details Over Generalizations

**General (Tell)**: "The house was old and neglected."

**Specific (Show)**: "Paint chips crunched under her feet. A rusty bike, half-swallowed by weeds, leaned against the porch railing."

---

## When Telling Is Better

Not everything needs to be shown. Tell when:

### Transitions
"Two weeks later, Sarah arrived in Boston."
(Don't show the entire journey if nothing important happens)

### Unimportant Information
"They ate dinner and went to bed."
(If dinner isn't significant, don't describe every bite)

### Pacing Needs Speed
Showing slows pace; telling accelerates it.
- Action sequence: More showing
- Getting character across town: More telling

### Already Established
If you've shown a trait multiple times, you can tell later.
"With her usual impatience, Maria interrupted."

### Clarity
Sometimes readers need direct information:
"The library was two blocks from her apartment."

---

## Finding the Balance

### The Iceberg Theory (Hemingway)

Show 1/8, imply 7/8. Trust readers to understand what's beneath the surface.

### Key Scenes vs. Summary

- **Key scenes**: Show fully with detail
- **Transitions**: Tell or summarize
- **Important emotions**: Show
- **Background emotions**: Can tell briefly

### The Revision Test

For each "telling" sentence, ask:
1. Is this important enough to show?
2. Can I show this through action, dialogue, or sensory detail?
3. Does the pacing allow for showing?
4. Have I already established this (can tell now)?

---

## Common Pitfalls

### Over-Telling Emotions

**Problem**: "She felt sad. She was so sad. The sadness overwhelmed her."

**Fix**: Show sadness through behavior, sensation, or dialogue.

### Double-Dipping

**Problem**: "'I hate you!' she said angrily."

**Fix**: The dialogue shows the anger; remove "angrily."

### Stage Direction

**Problem**: "He felt a pang of jealousy as he watched them together."

**Fix**: "His fingers tightened around his glass. They hadn't noticed him. She laughed at something the other man said—her real laugh, the one she'd stopped using with him months ago."

### Telling Internal State Directly

**Problem**: "She realized she loved him."

**Fix**: Show the moment of realization through her thoughts, sensations, and behavior. Let readers conclude she's realized her love.

## Practice Exercise

Transform these telling sentences into showing:

1. "He was tired."
2. "The food was delicious."
3. "She was worried about the test."
4. "They were in love."
5. "It was a beautiful sunset."

**Challenge**: Use different techniques for each (sensory details, action, dialogue, physical sensation, specific detail).

---

## Quick Checklist

Before finalizing prose:

- [ ] Are emotions shown through action and sensation?
- [ ] Are character traits revealed through behavior?
- [ ] Are relationships demonstrated through interaction?
- [ ] Is setting conveyed through specific, sensory detail?
- [ ] Are only appropriate things told (transitions, established traits, pacing needs)?
- [ ] No "double-dipping" (telling what dialogue already shows)?
- [ ] Trust readers to understand without explicit statements?
