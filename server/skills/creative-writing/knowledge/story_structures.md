# Story Structures Reference

Comprehensive guide to narrative structures for plotting and revising fiction.

---

## Three-Act Structure

The most fundamental Western storytelling structure.

### Act I: Setup (25%)

**Purpose**: Establish normal world, introduce characters, set up conflict

**Key Elements**:
- **Opening Image**: The first impression, sets tone
- **Ordinary World**: Protagonist's normal life
- **Introduction of Protagonist**: Show who they are before change
- **Establishing Stakes**: What matters to them
- **Inciting Incident** (10-12%): The event that disrupts normal
- **Debate**: Character hesitates or resists
- **First Plot Point** (25%): Point of no return, commits to journey

**Questions to Answer**:
- Who is the protagonist?
- What is their normal world?
- What do they want?
- What disrupts their world?
- Why can't they ignore it?

---

### Act II: Confrontation (50%)

**Purpose**: Complicate the journey, raise stakes, test the character

**First Half (Rising Action)**:
- **New World**: Character enters unfamiliar territory
- **Fun and Games**: The "promise of the premise"
- **B Story**: Subplot (often romance or friendship) that mirrors theme
- **Pinch Point 1** (37.5%): Antagonist shows strength
- **Midpoint** (50%): Major shift—false victory or false defeat

**Second Half (Deepening Conflict)**:
- **Raised Stakes**: Consequences become more severe
- **Pinch Point 2** (62.5%): Antagonist strikes again
- **All Is Lost** (75%): Lowest moment, often a "death"
- **Dark Night of the Soul**: Character processes loss

**Questions to Answer**:
- How do obstacles escalate?
- What does the character learn?
- What's the midpoint revelation?
- How do stakes increase?
- What's the low point?

---

### Act III: Resolution (25%)

**Purpose**: Resolve the conflict, show transformation

**Key Elements**:
- **Breakthrough**: Character realizes what they need
- **Gathering Forces**: Preparing for final battle
- **Climax**: Final confrontation with antagonist
- **Resolution**: Aftermath and new equilibrium
- **Final Image**: Mirrors opening image, shows change

**Questions to Answer**:
- What does the character finally understand?
- How do they face the final challenge?
- How have they changed?
- What's the new normal?

## The Hero's Journey (12 Stages)

Joseph Campbell's monomyth, expanded by Christopher Vogler.

### Departure

**1. Ordinary World**
The hero's normal life before the adventure. Establishes what will be transformed.

**2. Call to Adventure**
A challenge, problem, or opportunity disrupts the status quo.

**3. Refusal of the Call**
The hero hesitates, fears the unknown, or feels unworthy.

**4. Meeting with the Mentor**
A guide provides training, advice, or a gift for the journey.

**5. Crossing the First Threshold**
The hero commits to the adventure and enters the Special World.

### Initiation

**6. Tests, Allies, Enemies**
The hero faces challenges and learns the rules of the Special World. Allies and enemies are identified.

**7. Approach to the Inmost Cave**
The hero approaches the central crisis, often the antagonist's realm.

**8. The Ordeal**
The supreme crisis—death and rebirth moment. The hero faces their greatest fear.

**9. Reward (Seizing the Sword)**
After surviving, the hero takes possession of the treasure or achieves the goal.

### Return

**10. The Road Back**
The hero begins the journey home, often pursued or facing complications.

**11. Resurrection**
The final test—the hero must use everything learned. A "death and rebirth" at climax.

**12. Return with the Elixir**
The hero returns transformed, bringing something to benefit the ordinary world.

## Seven-Point Story Structure

Dan Wells' simplified structure.

1. **Hook**: Starting state (opposite of Resolution)
2. **Plot Turn 1**: Call to adventure, introduces conflict
3. **Pinch Point 1**: Pressure applied, antagonist shows strength
4. **Midpoint**: Character moves from reaction to action
5. **Pinch Point 2**: More pressure, things get worse
6. **Plot Turn 2**: Character gets final piece needed for victory
7. **Resolution**: Ending state (opposite of Hook)

**How to Use**:
1. Start with Resolution (where do you want to end?)
2. Create Hook (opposite of Resolution)
3. Place Midpoint (the shift between)
4. Add Plot Turns (what gets them from Hook to Midpoint, Midpoint to Resolution)
5. Add Pinch Points (what applies pressure)

## Freytag's Pyramid

Gustav Freytag's five-part dramatic structure.

```
           Climax
            /\
           /  \
          /    \
   Rising/      \Falling
  Action/        \Action
        /          \
       /            \
Exposition    Resolution/Denouement
```

1. **Exposition**: Background information, setup
2. **Rising Action**: Complications and conflict development
3. **Climax**: Turning point, highest tension
4. **Falling Action**: Events following climax
5. **Denouement/Resolution**: Final outcome

## Kishōtenketsu (Four-Act Structure)

East Asian structure that doesn't require conflict.

**Ki (起) - Introduction**
Introduce characters and setting without conflict

**Shō (承) - Development**
Develop the elements introduced, still without conflict

**Ten (転) - Twist**
An unexpected element is introduced that recontextualizes everything

**Ketsu (結) - Conclusion**
Elements are harmonized, reconciling the twist with what came before

**Best For**:
- Character studies
- Slice-of-life stories
- Literary fiction
- Stories about understanding rather than conflict

## Story Circle (Dan Harmon)

Simplified Hero's Journey in eight steps.

```
        1. Comfort Zone (You)
    8. Change          2. Want
        \              /
         \            /
  7. Return          3. Unfamiliar
           \        /
            \      /
     6. Take   4. Search
           \   /
            5. Find
```

1. **You**: Character in their zone of comfort
2. **Need**: But they want something
3. **Go**: They enter an unfamiliar situation
4. **Search**: Adapt to it, seek what they want
5. **Find**: Get what they wanted
6. **Take**: Pay a price for it
7. **Return**: Back to familiar situation
8. **Change**: Having changed

## The Story Spine

Pixar's storytelling formula.

- Once upon a time there was ___
- Every day, ___
- One day ___
- Because of that, ___
- Because of that, ___
- Because of that, ___
- Until finally ___
- And ever since then ___

## Choosing a Structure

### Consider:

**Genre Expectations**
- Thriller/Action: Strong three-act, clear beats
- Literary Fiction: More flexible, character-driven
- Romance: Often two POV, dual journey
- Mystery: Information reveal structure

**Story Type**
- Plot-driven: Tighter structure
- Character-driven: Looser structure
- Thematic: Structure serves theme

**Length**
- Short story: Streamlined structure
- Novel: Full structure with subplots
- Series: Multiple structures layered

### Remember:

Structure is a tool, not a rule. Use it to:
- Diagnose pacing problems
- Find missing elements
- Ensure emotional beats land
- Create satisfying reader experience

But don't let structure strangle organic storytelling.
