# Literary Devices Reference

A comprehensive guide to literary techniques for enriching prose.

---

## Figurative Language

### Simile

Comparison using "like" or "as"

**Examples**:
- "Her eyes were like two stars."
- "The news hit him like a punch to the stomach."
- "Life is like a box of chocolates."

**When to use**: When the comparison illuminates something; avoid clichéd similes.

---

### Metaphor

Direct comparison without "like" or "as"

**Examples**:
- "Time is money."
- "The world is a stage."
- "Her voice was honey."

**Extended Metaphor**: A metaphor developed throughout a passage or entire work.

---

### Personification

Giving human qualities to non-human things

**Examples**:
- "The wind whispered through the trees."
- "Fear gripped his heart."
- "The city never sleeps."

---

### Hyperbole

Deliberate exaggeration for effect

**Examples**:
- "I've told you a million times."
- "I'm so hungry I could eat a horse."
- "The bag weighed a ton."

---

### Understatement (Litotes)

Deliberate minimization for effect

**Examples**:
- "It's just a scratch." (about a serious wound)
- "He's not the brightest bulb."
- "That went well." (after disaster)

---

### Oxymoron

Combining contradictory terms

**Examples**:
- "Deafening silence"
- "Bittersweet"
- "Living dead"
- "Cruel kindness"

---

### Paradox

A statement that contradicts itself but reveals a truth

**Examples**:
- "The more you know, the more you know you don't know."
- "You have to be cruel to be kind."
- "This is the beginning of the end."

---

## Sound Devices

### Alliteration

Repetition of initial consonant sounds

**Examples**:
- "Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers."
- "The fair breeze blew, the white foam flew."
- "She sells seashells by the seashore."

---

### Assonance

Repetition of vowel sounds

**Examples**:
- "The rain in Spain falls mainly on the plain."
- "Fleet feet sweep by sleeping geese."
- "Hear the mellow wedding bells."

---

### Consonance

Repetition of consonant sounds (not at beginning)

**Examples**:
- "Pitter-patter"
- "All mammals named Sam are clammy."
- "Mike likes his bike."

---

### Onomatopoeia

Words that imitate sounds

**Examples**:
- "Buzz," "hiss," "boom," "crack"
- "The bees buzzed busily."
- "Thunder crashed overhead."

---

### Euphony

Pleasant, harmonious sounds

**Example**: "Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness"

---

### Cacophony

Harsh, discordant sounds for effect

**Example**: "The clash and clang of steel on steel"

---

## Structural Devices

### Anaphora

Repetition of words at the beginning of successive clauses

**Example**:
"We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields."

---

### Epistrophe

Repetition of words at the end of successive clauses

**Example**:
"Government of the people, by the people, for the people."

---

### Parallelism

Similar grammatical structure in related phrases

**Example**:
"Easy come, easy go."
"I came, I saw, I conquered."

---

### Antithesis

Juxtaposition of contrasting ideas in balanced phrases

**Examples**:
- "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times."
- "Give me liberty, or give me death."
- "To err is human; to forgive, divine."

---

### Chiasmus

Reversed parallelism (ABBA structure)

**Examples**:
- "Ask not what your country can do for you; ask what you can do for your country."
- "Never let a fool kiss you or a kiss fool you."

---

### Juxtaposition

Placing contrasting elements side by side

**Example**: A beautiful flower growing in a garbage dump. Wealth next to poverty.

---

## Narrative Devices

### Foreshadowing

Hints about future events

**Types**:
- **Direct**: Clear hint ("Little did she know...")
- **Indirect**: Symbolic or subtle
- **Red herring**: False foreshadowing

---

### Flashback

Scene set earlier than main narrative

**Uses**:
- Reveal character backstory
- Explain motivations
- Create mystery
- Build suspense

---

### In Medias Res

Starting in the middle of action

**Benefits**:
- Immediate engagement
- Creates questions
- Shows character under pressure

---

### Frame Narrative

Story within a story

**Examples**:
- A character telling a story
- Letters or journal entries
- Multiple nested narratives

---

### Unreliable Narrator

Narrator whose account may not be trustworthy

**Types**:
- Intentionally deceptive
- Self-deluded
- Naive or limited understanding
- Mentally unstable

---

### Stream of Consciousness

Continuous flow of thoughts and feelings

**Characteristics**:
- Associative logic
- Fragments and run-ons
- Sensory impressions
- No traditional structure

---

## Irony

### Verbal Irony

Saying the opposite of what is meant

**Example**: "What lovely weather!" during a storm.

---

### Situational Irony

Outcome opposite to what was expected

**Example**: A fire station burns down.

---

### Dramatic Irony

Reader knows something characters don't

**Example**: Reader knows the killer's identity; detective doesn't.

---

## Symbolic Devices

### Symbolism

Object/image representing something beyond itself

**Common Symbols**:
- Water: Purification, life, change
- Light/Dark: Good/evil, knowledge/ignorance
- Seasons: Life cycles, change
- Journey: Life, transformation

---

### Motif

Recurring element that develops theme

**Examples**:
- Blood in Macbeth (guilt)
- Eyes in The Great Gatsby (observation/judgment)
- Windows (opportunity, barrier)

---

### Allegory

Extended metaphor where entire narrative represents something else

**Examples**:
- Animal Farm (Soviet Russia)
- The Chronicles of Narnia (Christianity)
- Pilgrim's Progress (Christian life)

---

### Allusion

Reference to another work, event, person, or place

**Types**:
- Literary: "He was a real Romeo."
- Biblical: "She carried her cross."
- Historical: "It was his Waterloo."
- Mythological: "Her Achilles' heel."

---

## Rhetorical Devices

### Rhetorical Question

Question asked for effect, not answer

**Example**: "What's in a name?"

---

### Apostrophe

Addressing absent/imaginary person or abstraction

**Example**: "O Death, where is thy sting?"

---

### Epigraph

Quotation at beginning of work

**Purpose**: Set tone, establish theme, create framework

---

### Deus Ex Machina

Unexpected solution to unsolvable problem

**Note**: Generally considered weak writing unless intentional/ironic.

---

## Tone and Mood Devices

### Pathetic Fallacy

Nature reflecting character emotions

**Example**: Storm during character's anger; sunshine when happy.

---

### Atmosphere

Overall feeling created by setting and description

**Elements**:
- Setting details
- Weather
- Light/darkness
- Sound
- Sensory details

---

## Quick Reference by Effect

### To Create Tension
- Short sentences
- Foreshadowing
- Dramatic irony
- Withholding information

### To Create Humor
- Irony (all types)
- Hyperbole
- Understatement
- Incongruity

### To Create Beauty
- Imagery
- Euphony
- Metaphor
- Parallelism

### To Emphasize Ideas
- Repetition (anaphora, epistrophe)
- Antithesis
- Parallelism
- Rhetorical questions

### To Create Depth
- Symbolism
- Motif
- Allusion
- Subtext

---

## Usage Notes

### Best Practices

1. **Purposeful**: Every device should serve the story
2. **Invisible**: Technique shouldn't overshadow content
3. **Fresh**: Avoid clichéd uses
4. **Appropriate**: Match technique to tone and genre
5. **Restrained**: Don't overuse any single device

### Warning Signs

- Technique calls attention to itself
- Reader is confused, not engaged
- Devices feel forced or artificial
- Too many techniques competing
