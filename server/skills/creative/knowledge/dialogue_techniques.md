# Dialogue Techniques

Craft authentic, purposeful dialogue that reveals character and drives story.

---

## What Good Dialogue Does

### The Four Functions

1. **Reveals Character**: How people speak shows who they are
2. **Advances Plot**: Dialogue moves the story forward
3. **Provides Information**: Naturally conveys what readers need to know
4. **Creates Tension**: Generates or escalates conflict

Every line of dialogue should do at least one of these—ideally more.

---

## Making Dialogue Sound Real (Without Being Real)

### Real Speech vs. Written Dialogue

Real conversations include:
- Um, uh, well, so, like
- Incomplete sentences
- Interruptions
- Topic changes
- Repetition
- Small talk

Written dialogue is:
- Compressed (no filler)
- Purposeful (serves the story)
- Cleaner (but not too perfect)
- Distinct (characters sound different)

**The goal**: Create the *illusion* of real speech without actual real speech.

### Reading Aloud

Always read dialogue aloud. If it sounds:
- **Stilted**: Loosen it up
- **Too formal**: Add contractions, fragments
- **Confusing**: Clarify attribution
- **All the same**: Differentiate voices

## Character Voice

### Making Characters Sound Distinct

**Vocabulary Level**
- Education and background affect word choice
- "I don't understand" vs. "I'm confused" vs. "What?"

**Sentence Structure**
- Short, choppy sentences vs. flowing, complex ones
- Questions vs. statements
- Fragments vs. complete sentences

**Speech Patterns**
- Favorite phrases or verbal tics
- Interrupting vs. waiting to speak
- Direct vs. roundabout communication

**What They Talk About**
- What topics do they return to?
- What do they avoid discussing?
- How do they deflect?

### Voice Exercise

Can you tell who's speaking without dialogue tags?

**Character A**: "The optimal strategy requires a multi-phase approach. First—"

**Character B**: "Yeah, no, we're not doing that."

**Character C**: "I mean... what if we just, you know, talked to them?"

## Subtext: What's Not Said

### The Iceberg Principle

Surface dialogue often hides deeper meaning.

**Surface**: "Nice weather we're having."
**Subtext**: "I don't know what to say to you anymore."

### Types of Subtext

**Deflection**
```
"How was the doctor's appointment?"
"Traffic was terrible getting home."
```

**Coded Language**
```
"How's your 'friend' doing?"
(We both know it's more than a friend)
```

**Loaded Questions**
```
"Working late again?"
(I know you're not really working)
```

**Misdirection**
```
"I'm fine."
(I am absolutely not fine)
```

### Creating Subtext

Ask: What does this character *really* want to say but can't or won't?

**Why they can't say it**:
- Social convention
- Power dynamics
- Protecting themselves
- Protecting others
- Fear of consequences
- Don't have the words

## Conflict in Dialogue

### Types of Dialogue Conflict

**Direct Opposition**
```
"We need to sell the house."
"I'll never sell this house."
```

**Indirect Opposition**
```
"We need to talk about the house."
"Did I tell you Sarah called?"
```

**Competing Agendas**
```
"I thought we could go out tonight."
"I have a lot of work."
"You always have work."
```

**Power Struggles**
```
"I'm recommending we delay the launch."
"That's noted. Anything else?"
```

### Escalation Techniques

1. **Increase directness**: Subtext becomes text
2. **Raise stakes**: What's at risk gets bigger
3. **Narrow options**: Fewer ways out
4. **Break rules**: Characters say what's normally unsaid
5. **Physical action**: Dialogue moves to action

## Dialogue Beats and Pacing

### What Are Beats?

Beats are the small units of action, description, or silence between dialogue lines.

### Using Beats

**Slow down important moments**
```
"I'm leaving." She set down her wedding ring on the counter. "Tonight."
```

**Control rhythm**
```
"I can't do this."
Silence.
"Did you hear me?"
"I heard you."
```

**Avoid "talking heads"**
```
"The project's behind schedule." Marcus tapped his pen against the table.
"How far behind?"
"Three weeks." He met her eyes. "Maybe four."
```

### Pacing Principles

- **Fast exchanges**: For urgency, tension, comedy
- **Beats between**: For weight, reflection, tension
- **Longer speeches**: For revelation, emotion, authority

## Exposition Through Dialogue

### The "As You Know, Bob" Problem

Bad exposition:
```
"As you know, Bob, we've been partners for ten years, and our company makes software for hospitals."
```

Why would Bob be told what he knows?

### Better Exposition Techniques

**Conflict as Vehicle**
```
"Ten years, Bob. I put ten years into this company."
"And I put in eleven. What's your point?"
```

**New Character**
Let someone who doesn't know ask questions.

**Discovery**
```
"What is this place?"
"The lab where it all started. Before the accident."
```

**Argument About Facts**
```
"That's not how it happened."
"I was there. I remember exactly how it happened."
```

**Partial Information**
Don't explain everything. Let readers piece it together.

## Internal Dialogue (Thoughts)

### When to Use

- Showing immediate reaction
- Revealing hidden feelings
- Creating irony (thoughts vs. words)
- Deepening POV

### Formatting Options

**Italics without tags**
```
She smiled at him. What is he hiding?
```

**With thought tags**
```
What is he hiding? she wondered.
```

**Integrated (free indirect discourse)**
```
She smiled at him. He was definitely hiding something. She could always tell.
```

### Best Practices

- Use sparingly (don't over-explain)
- Thoughts should feel natural to the character
- Don't repeat what dialogue already shows
- Can create dramatic irony

## Dialogue Tags and Action Beats

### Tags

**"Said" is invisible** - use it freely
**"Asked"** - fine for questions
**Avoid purple tags** - "exclaimed," "ejaculated," "pronounced"

### When to Cut Tags

When it's clear who's speaking:
```
"I can't believe you did that."
"I had no choice."
"There's always a choice."
```

### Action Beats

Replace tags with action for variety:
```
"I can't do this." She stood and walked to the window.
"You don't have a choice." Marcus didn't look up from his papers.
```

## Common Dialogue Mistakes

### On-the-Nose Dialogue

Characters say exactly what they mean with no subtext.

**Problem**: "I'm angry at you because you forgot my birthday, which makes me feel unimportant to you."

**Better**: "Busy day?"
"The usual. Why?"
"No reason."

### Maid-and-Butler Dialogue

Characters explain things they both already know.

**Problem**: "I'll get the car, the blue sedan we bought last year after your accident."

**Fix**: Cut or give a reason for the explanation.

### All Characters Sound the Same

**Fix**: Give each character distinct vocabulary, rhythm, concerns, speech patterns.

### Too Perfect

No one interrupts, misspeaks, or talks past each other.

**Fix**: Add realistic imperfection—but purposefully.

### Dialect Overload

Phonetic spelling makes dialogue hard to read.

**Better**: Suggest accent with word choice and rhythm, not spelling.

## Revision Checklist

- [ ] Each line reveals character, advances plot, or creates tension
- [ ] Subtext is present where appropriate
- [ ] Characters sound distinct from each other
- [ ] No "as you know, Bob" exposition
- [ ] Tags are mostly "said" or cut entirely
- [ ] Action beats add variety and meaning
- [ ] Dialogue reads naturally aloud
- [ ] Conflict or tension is present
- [ ] Pacing is controlled through beats
- [ ] Internal dialogue is used sparingly and effectively
