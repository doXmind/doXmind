# Structured Writing: 5 Frameworks for Clear Logic

The difference between writing that persuades and writing that confuses often comes down to structure. You can have brilliant insights, compelling evidence, and elegant prose -- but if the pieces are arranged in the wrong order, readers will struggle to follow your argument.

Structure is the skeleton of your writing. It determines how ideas connect, how arguments build, and how readers experience your logic. A strong structure makes complex ideas feel simple. A weak structure makes simple ideas feel complex.

This guide introduces five proven writing frameworks that you can apply immediately to any document. Each framework is a different way of organizing information, suited to different purposes and audiences. Learn all five, and you will always have a clear path from blank page to finished draft.

## Framework 1: SCQA (Situation, Complication, Question, Answer)

SCQA is one of the most versatile frameworks in professional writing. Developed by Barbara Minto at McKinsey, it is designed to lead readers from a shared understanding to a clear conclusion.

### How It Works

- **Situation:** Establish the context that your reader already knows and agrees with.
- **Complication:** Introduce the problem, change, or tension that disrupts the situation.
- **Question:** Articulate the question that the complication naturally raises.
- **Answer:** Deliver your solution, recommendation, or thesis.

### Why It Works

SCQA works because it mirrors how humans naturally process information. We start with what we know (situation), encounter a problem (complication), feel the need to resolve it (question), and seek resolution (answer). By structuring your writing this way, you create a pull that draws readers forward.

### Practical Example

Imagine you are writing a proposal for adopting a new project management tool.

> **Situation:** Our team has grown from 8 to 35 people in the past year, and we manage all projects through a combination of spreadsheets and email threads.
>
> **Complication:** As the team has scaled, this approach has broken down. Tasks are being duplicated, deadlines are missed because updates are buried in email, and new hires spend their first week just trying to understand where to find project information.
>
> **Question:** How do we create a project management system that scales with our team and keeps everyone aligned?
>
> **Answer:** We should adopt a dedicated project management platform -- specifically, one that offers kanban boards, timeline views, and integrations with our existing tools. After evaluating five options, I recommend Tool X for the following reasons...

The rest of the document then supports the answer with evidence, comparisons, and implementation details.

### Best Used For

Executive summaries, business proposals, consulting reports, recommendation memos, and any writing where you need to build a case for a specific action.

## Framework 2: The Pyramid Principle

Also developed by Barbara Minto, the Pyramid Principle takes a top-down approach to organizing information. You start with the conclusion and then support it with increasingly detailed layers of evidence.

### How It Works

- **Lead with the main point.** State your conclusion, recommendation, or key insight first.
- **Support with key arguments.** Group your supporting points into three to five main arguments.
- **Back each argument with evidence.** Under each argument, provide data, examples, or analysis.

The structure forms a pyramid: one point at the top, a few supporting arguments in the middle, and many pieces of evidence at the base.

### Why It Works

Busy readers -- executives, managers, clients -- want the answer before the analysis. The Pyramid Principle respects their time by front-loading the conclusion. Readers who are convinced can stop early. Readers who need more detail can continue deeper into the pyramid.

### Practical Example

You are writing a report on whether to expand into a new market.

> **Main point:** We should enter the Southeast Asian market in Q3, starting with Singapore and Vietnam.
>
> **Argument 1: Market demand is strong.**
> - E-commerce spending in Southeast Asia grew 20% last year.
> - Our product category is underserved in the region.
> - Survey data shows 73% of target users would consider switching to our product.
>
> **Argument 2: We have a competitive advantage.**
> - Our multilingual platform already supports Vietnamese and Malay.
> - Competitors have not yet established a presence in the region.
> - Our pricing model aligns well with local purchasing power.
>
> **Argument 3: The financial risk is manageable.**
> - Initial investment is estimated at $200K, with break-even projected within 9 months.
> - We can leverage existing infrastructure in Japan as a regional hub.
> - Downside scenario still shows a positive return within 18 months.

### Best Used For

Business reports, executive briefings, strategy documents, research summaries, and any writing where the reader wants the conclusion upfront.

## Framework 3: Problem-Solution

The Problem-Solution framework is the workhorse of persuasive writing. It is simple, direct, and effective across almost every genre.

### How It Works

- **Define the problem.** Describe the pain point, challenge, or gap in vivid, concrete terms.
- **Present the solution.** Explain what you propose and how it addresses the problem.
- **Show the evidence.** Demonstrate that the solution works through data, case studies, or logical argument.
- **Address objections.** Anticipate counterarguments and respond to them.
- **Call to action.** Tell the reader what to do next.

### Why It Works

Humans are wired to seek solutions to problems. When you articulate a problem that the reader recognizes, you create an immediate desire for resolution. The rest of your writing satisfies that desire.

### Practical Example

You are writing a blog post about developer burnout.

> **Problem:** Developer burnout is reaching epidemic proportions. A 2024 survey found that 83% of software developers experience burnout symptoms, with 40% considering leaving the industry entirely. The primary drivers are unrealistic deadlines, constant context-switching, and a culture that equates long hours with commitment.
>
> **Solution:** Teams need to adopt sustainable development practices that protect deep work, set realistic expectations, and measure output rather than hours. Three specific changes make the biggest difference...
>
> **Evidence:** Companies that have implemented these practices report 35% lower turnover, 28% higher code quality metrics, and -- counter-intuitively -- faster delivery times.
>
> **Objections:** "We cannot afford to slow down." In reality, burnout is far more expensive than sustainable pacing. The cost of replacing a burned-out developer averages 1.5 to 2 times their annual salary.
>
> **Call to action:** Start with one change this sprint. Pick the lowest-effort, highest-impact practice from this article and implement it. Measure the results after four weeks.

### Best Used For

Blog posts, opinion pieces, product marketing, grant proposals, policy recommendations, and any writing that argues for change.

## Framework 4: Chronological Narrative

Sometimes the most effective structure is also the most natural: tell the story in the order it happened.

### How It Works

- **Set the scene.** Establish the starting point -- the initial conditions, the context, the "before."
- **Trace the journey.** Walk through events, decisions, and turning points in sequence.
- **Arrive at the outcome.** Describe where things stand now and what was learned.
- **Extract the lesson.** Articulate the takeaway that makes the story worth telling.

### Why It Works

Humans are storytelling creatures. A chronological narrative creates suspense (what happens next?), empathy (the reader lives through the experience vicariously), and memorability (stories stick in the mind far longer than abstract arguments).

### Practical Example

You are writing a case study about a product launch.

> **Scene:** In January 2024, our team was tasked with launching a new feature that our largest client had been requesting for two years. We had a three-month deadline and a team of four.
>
> **Journey:** The first two weeks were spent in discovery, interviewing the client and mapping their workflow. By week three, we had a prototype. By week five, we hit our first major obstacle: the feature required an API integration that our vendor had deprecated. We spent a tense week exploring alternatives before finding a workaround that was actually more elegant than the original plan...
>
> **Outcome:** We launched on time, the client renewed their contract, and the feature became our most-used addition in Q2.
>
> **Lesson:** The constraint that almost derailed us -- the deprecated API -- forced us to find a better architecture. Sometimes the obstacle is the path.

### Best Used For

Case studies, postmortems, project retrospectives, personal essays, process documentation, and any writing where the sequence of events matters.

## Framework 5: Compare and Contrast

When your writing involves evaluating options, explaining differences, or helping readers make decisions, the compare-and-contrast framework provides clarity.

### How It Works

There are two main approaches:

**Point-by-point:** For each criterion, compare all options before moving to the next criterion.

**Block method:** Describe one option completely, then the next, then synthesize.

In both cases, the structure includes:

- **Establish criteria.** Define the dimensions you will compare.
- **Analyze each option.** Evaluate systematically against the criteria.
- **Synthesize.** Draw conclusions from the comparison.

### Practical Example (Point-by-Point)

You are writing a technical evaluation of two database options.

> **Performance under load:**
> PostgreSQL handles complex queries efficiently due to its advanced query planner but requires careful tuning for write-heavy workloads. MongoDB excels at write throughput with its append-only storage model but can struggle with complex aggregations at scale.
>
> **Developer experience:**
> PostgreSQL requires SQL knowledge and schema design upfront, which adds initial overhead but pays off in data integrity. MongoDB's schemaless approach enables rapid prototyping but can lead to data inconsistencies if not carefully managed.
>
> **Operational complexity:**
> PostgreSQL benefits from decades of tooling and community knowledge. MongoDB requires more specialized operational expertise, particularly for sharding and replica set management.
>
> **Synthesis:** For our use case -- a read-heavy application with complex relational data -- PostgreSQL is the stronger choice. If we were building a real-time event ingestion pipeline, MongoDB would have the edge.

### Best Used For

Product comparisons, technology evaluations, literature reviews, policy analysis, and any writing that helps readers understand the trade-offs between options.

## Choosing the Right Framework

The best framework depends on your purpose and audience:

| Framework | Best When You Need To... |
|---|---|
| SCQA | Build a case from shared context to a specific recommendation |
| Pyramid Principle | Deliver conclusions first, with supporting detail on demand |
| Problem-Solution | Persuade readers that a change is needed and your approach is right |
| Chronological | Tell a story, build empathy, or document a process |
| Compare-Contrast | Help readers evaluate options and make decisions |

You can also combine frameworks. A report might use the Pyramid Principle at the document level while individual sections use Problem-Solution or Compare-Contrast internally.
